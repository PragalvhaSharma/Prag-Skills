What is going on

RegionFly has flown the same seven routes along the eastern seaboard since 1971, and deliberately never grew. That worked for four decades. Then fares fell, demand softened, and by 2017 the company was profitable but going backwards, so a larger airline bought it.

The new owners want costs down. Two routes were already cancelled after 2016, and profit fell anyway. Now the owners are asking which routes to cut next, and they want an answer in three days.

Ed and Fabiana have to tell them. The trouble is that the only numbers they have are route profits produced by a cost system that spreads overhead on revenue, so the busiest routes look like the worst ones.

Start from what actually happens

1

RegionFly flies planes between small cities. A plane leaves the gate with a fixed number of seats on it, and it costs the same to fly whether those seats are full or empty.

2

Money comes in one way: somebody buys a seat. RegionFly does not set that fare. The majors and the other regionals do, and RegionFly takes what the market gives.

Route #5 fares, 2018 = $608,818
3

Some money goes out because that flight happened. Burn the fuel, pay the crew for those hours, pay the airport to land. No flight, no cost.

Route #5, 2018 = $98,196 16 cents of every fare dollar
4

The rest goes out whether the plane moves or not. The lease on the aircraft, the hangar, the head office. Cancel every flight tomorrow and this bill still arrives.

Whole airline, 2018 = $2,179,686 no single route causes any of it
→

So dropping a route loses you all of its fares and saves you only the fuel and crew. The big bill stays.

What this business has to be good at

◐ Fill the seats. The fixed bill is the same either way, so every extra passenger is nearly pure profit. Slipping: revenue per route is falling faster than seats flown.
✕ Keep the planes flying. A parked aircraft still costs its full lease. Two routes were cancelled in 2017 and the lease bill did not move.
✕ Know what a route really costs. Every keep or kill call runs off this number. The system spreads overhead on revenue, so the biggest route looks the most expensive by construction.
Which means Three quarters of this airline's costs do not care what you fly. So the only number that answers a keep or kill question is fares minus the costs that leave with the flight. The reported profit per route, which has a share of the $2.18M buried in it, answers a different question and will give you the wrong answer to this one.

Rate = Overhead to spread  ÷  What you spread it on
     = $2,179,686  ÷  $4,164,340
     = 0.5234, i.e. 52.34 cents of overhead per dollar of fares
The part nobody says out loud Nothing in the accounts told anyone to divide by fares. Somebody chose it. A route does not consume head office in proportion to what it charges passengers, so this rate quietly taxes the busiest routes hardest, then the resulting margin gets used to decide which routes to cut.
