# Formats

## X

The feed is a hostile environment. The first line decides everything; there is no benefit of the doubt and no scroll-back.

**Rules**

- **One idea per post.** Not one topic — one move.
- **First line does all the work.** It must be the claim or a concrete detail. Never context, never setup, never a question.
- **Default to a single post.** A thread is only justified when the idea needs *sequence* — steps that depend on each other. An idea chopped into six posts for reach reads as exactly that.
- **It stands alone.** A post excerpted from an essay must make sense to someone who will never click the link. Rewrite it for the feed; don't paste a paragraph.
- **No hashtags, no emoji, no 🧵, no "Thread:", no "Let that sink in", no reply-farming questions.**
- **No link in the first post** of a thread. Put it at the end, once the reader has a reason.

**Length.** Aim under 280 so it renders without a "show more" fold. Longer is possible on his account, but a post that folds loses most of its readers at the fold — if it needs the length, it needs to be an essay.

**Thread shape, when a thread is genuinely warranted**

1. Post 1 is the whole claim, standing alone. If post 1 doesn't work as a single tweet, the thread is dead.
2. Each following post is one complete thought. No sentences continuing across posts.
3. No "1/7" counters, no numbering, no recap posts.
4. Last post lands — the sharpest implication, not a summary and not a CTA. Link goes here if there is one.

**Good first lines** are flat and strange enough to stop a scroll: a specific number, a thing he was wrong about, a blunt assertion someone would argue with.

## Substack

**Title.** A claim or a concrete noun phrase. Two to six words is usually enough. No colon-subtitle construction — pick the half that's concrete and drop the other. No gerund how-to titles ("Building Better AI Tools"). No numbered listicle framing unless the piece really is a list.

Offer 3–5 options, ranked, best first, each with a few words on what it promises the reader.

**Subtitle field.** One sentence that *adds* — a stake, a limit, or the surprise. Never a restatement of the title.

**Subheadings.** None under about 1200 words. A short piece with subheadings looks like a memo and tells the reader the prose can't carry them. Past 1200, use them sparingly, and make them assertions rather than labels ("Judgment is the expensive part", not "Background").

**Opening.** Same rule as everywhere: start in the middle. The first sentence should be interesting alone. Substack shows the first line in email previews, so it is also doing the job of a headline.

**Length.** Whatever the thought takes. 600–1500 is the usual band. A tight 500-word piece is better than a padded 1200-word one, and readers notice which one they got.

**Footnotes.** Up to three, for real tangents and honest caveats. See voice-and-style.md.

**Images.** Optional. Never stock photography, never AI-generated decoration. A real screenshot, chart, or photo of the actual thing, or nothing.

**Ending.** The sharpest implication, one step past the body. Short, plain, slightly cold.

**Companion X post.** Every essay ships with one drafted alongside it, written for the feed rather than excerpted, with the link at the end.

## Both

When `target: both`, write the essay first and derive the post. Deriving an essay from a post produces padding; deriving a post from an essay produces compression, which is what you want.

The post is not a teaser. Give away the actual point — people who want the argument will click for the argument.
