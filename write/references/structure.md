# Structure — the coherence machine

> **Editing, not composing.** This file is the standard for judging whether his
> argument holds. Use it to find the claim *in his material* and to name gaps back
> to him as questions. Never write a missing step yourself. See non-negotiable 1.
Coherence is not a writing quality. It's an architectural one. A coherent essay is one where each paragraph makes the next paragraph necessary, and you can't remove a step without the end falling over. That's built before prose, in outline form, where the gaps are still visible.

## The claim

One sentence. 20 words or fewer. Written down.

A claim has to be **contestable** and **specific**.

| Not a claim | Why | Fixed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI is changing how we work | Nobody disagrees | Most AI tools at work fail because they automate the wrong half of the job |
| Ambition matters | Platitude | Ambition is a skill you practice on small things, not a trait you have |
| I learned a lot at Opendoor | Diary entry | Operations work is where you find out which AI problems are real |

Two tests:

1. **The shrug test.** Read it to a smart reader. Would they disagree, or shrug? A shrug is fatal.
2. **The dinner test.** Would he defend this out loud, with a specific reason, to someone who pushed back? If not, it isn't his claim yet — it's a phrase.

If the transcript contains three contestable claims, that's three essays. Pick the one he has the most concrete material for and put the others in `ideas.md`.

## The chain

4–8 steps. Each step is **a complete sentence that asserts something**, not a topic label.

```
Bad (topic labels — these hide the gaps)
1. Intro to the problem
2. Background on AI tools
3. My experience
4. What this means

Good (claims — the gaps are visible)
1. Most AI-at-work projects target the visible part of a job, because that's the part that's easy to describe.
2. The visible part is usually the cheap part. The expensive part is the judgment nobody wrote down.
3. At Opendoor the paint-scoping bottleneck wasn't ordering paint, it was deciding what condition the paint was in.
4. So the automation that worked started by encoding the judgment, and treated the ordering as an afterthought.
5. Which inverts the usual advice: don't automate the repetitive step, automate the step people argue about.
```

### The seam test

For every adjacent pair of steps, read N then N+1 and ask: **does this follow, or did I skip a move?**

Skipped moves are the single most common reason writing feels slick but hollow. The reader can't say what's wrong; they just stop believing you. When you find a gap, either insert the missing step or cut the leap.

### The objection pass

Name the strongest counterargument to the claim — the one a smart, unfriendly reader makes. Then either answer it in the piece or narrow the claim until it's true.

Do not answer a weak version of the objection. A straw man is more damaging than silence, because readers who hold the real objection now know you're not arguing in good faith.

### The removal test

Delete any step. Does the conclusion still stand? If yes, that step was decoration. Cut it for real.

## Shapes that work

- **The reframe.** Everyone thinks X is about A. It's actually about B, and here's what follows. Strongest shape; needs a genuine reframe, not a synonym.
- **The mechanism.** Everyone agrees X happens. Here is *why*, in steps. Good when he has inside knowledge.
- **The consequence.** Accept this uncontroversial premise. Follow it three steps further than anyone does, and it lands somewhere uncomfortable.
- **The case.** Here is one specific thing I did or watched, in detail, and the general lesson it forced. Needs real specifics — the detail *is* the argument.
- **The taxonomy.** There are three kinds of X. Usually boring. Only use it when the categories themselves are the surprise.

## Discovery

An essay's job is to work something out, not to present a conclusion he already had. If the whole thing was known before writing, it's a post, and it should be short.

The practical version: while outlining, if a step turns out to be more interesting than the claim, **change the claim**. Following the interesting thing is not a detour; it's the method. PG's essays feel alive because he visibly changes his mind mid-argument. Fixed-outline writing reads like a memo.

## Length

The piece ends when the thought ends. No padding to hit a number, no compressing a real argument to look punchy.

Rough bands, for reference only: an X post is one move. A short Substack piece (400–700 words) is one claim with one example. A full essay (900–1800) is a chain with an objection handled. Past 2000, check that it isn't two essays holding hands.
