# Voice and style — the surface

> **Editing, not composing.** These are the mechanics good prose has, used to decide
> what to cut and what to fix in his sentences. They are not a style to write in on
> his behalf. His phrasing beats a more elegant one of yours every time.
Read `~/Documents/Writing/voice/VOICE.md` first. It wins over everything here.

This file is the general grammar: how Paul Graham's prose actually works, mechanically, so it can be applied deliberately instead of imitated.

## Register

Write as if talking to one specific smart friend who knows nothing about the topic and will interrupt if you're boring. Not an audience. Not "readers." One person.

That gives you the whole register for free: contractions, "you" and "I", short words, no performance. It also kills every corporate reflex, because you would never say "leverage" to a friend.

## Diction

- **Shortest word that's exact.** Not the shortest word — the shortest *exact* one. "Use" beats "utilize" every time; "approximate" does not beat "rough" if you mean rough.
- **Anglo-Saxon over Latinate.** Get, make, use, break, show, keep — over obtain, generate, utilize, fracture, demonstrate, maintain.
- **No jargon without a plain-English gloss** in the same sentence. If the gloss is hard to write, the idea isn't clear yet.
- **Zero words for emphasis.** Very, really, truly, genuinely, incredibly, significantly, dramatically. They make a claim weaker by admitting it needs propping up. Delete and let the noun carry it.
- **Plain verbs, no nominalizations.** "We decided" not "we made the decision." "It failed" not "there was a failure."

## Sentences

Default shape is subject–verb–object, front-loaded, one clause doing one job. Then break it on purpose.

**Rhythm is the main tool.** The pattern that does most of the work:

> A longer sentence that lays out the situation with its qualifications and its clauses and gives the reader something to hold. Then a short one. It lands.

Long, long, short. The short sentence carries the point, and it only works because the long ones earned the pause. A page of all-short sentences is just as flat as a page of all-long ones — and it's the more common failure, because short sentences feel like style.

**Lumpiness is the signature of a human.** Paragraphs run 1 to 6 sentences. Some are one line. Sentence lengths vary from 3 words to 40. Uniform rhythm is the most reliable tell of machine prose, and it survives every other edit, so check it on purpose.

## Concrete before abstract

Never state a principle before showing the thing it came from. The order is: specific case, then the general point. Reversing it turns an essay into a lecture.

Every abstraction gets a specific within one or two sentences — real numbers, a real name, a real moment. If there isn't one available, mark `[EXAMPLE?]` and ask rather than writing a plausible-sounding generic one.

Scale matters here: "$100k of weekly paint orders" does work that "significant operational volume" cannot.

## Surprise is the requirement

The piece has to tell the reader something they didn't already believe. That's the whole bar. Well-constructed agreement is not an essay; it's flattery.

Check it directly: after each section, ask what the reader now believes that they didn't three paragraphs ago. If the answer is "nothing, but stated nicely," cut the section.

## Two kinds of hedging

This is the distinction people miss when imitating PG. He qualifies constantly — and it makes him *more* credible, not less, because his qualifications are precise.

**Good (precision — narrows the claim to what's true):**
- "at least in the cases I've seen"
- "this is true of startups, less so of big companies"
- "I'd guess three out of four, though I haven't counted"

**Bad (cowardice — dodges responsibility for the claim):**
- "arguably", "it could be argued", "some might say", "many would agree"
- "I think" sprinkled on assertions he's willing to defend
- "in my opinion" on something that is obviously his opinion

Rule: a hedge that tells the reader *where the claim stops* stays. A hedge that only protects the writer goes.

## Openers

Start in the middle of the thought. The first sentence should be interesting standing alone, with no context.

Kill, on sight:
- Definitions of a common term
- "In today's world" / "In an era of" / "As AI becomes more..."
- Scene-setting about how long he's been thinking about this
- Any sentence whose only job is to announce the topic
- A rhetorical question

Usually the real opener is already in the transcript, three minutes in, in a sentence he threw away.

Openers that work: a flat surprising assertion; a specific moment with no preamble; something he was wrong about.

## Endings

Do not summarize. The reader just read it.

Land on the sharpest implication of the argument — one step further than the body went, so the last line gives them something to carry out. Understatement beats crescendo. No call to action, no "so go build something," no inspirational turn.

Best last lines are short, plain, and slightly cold.

## Paragraphs

One move each. A paragraph that makes two moves is two paragraphs; a paragraph that makes none is deleted.

No transition words doing the work a logical connection should do. If "furthermore" or "additionally" is needed, the two paragraphs aren't actually connected — fix the chain, don't bridge it with a word.

## Footnotes

PG uses them heavily and Substack supports them. Use one for a tangent that's genuinely interesting but would break the chain, or for the honest caveat that would clutter the sentence. If a footnote is load-bearing for the argument, it isn't a footnote — promote it into the body.

Cap: three per piece. More than that and the essay is fighting itself.

## The read-aloud test

Read the finished draft out loud, all of it. Every place you stumble is a real defect — usually a clause in the wrong order or a word he'd never say. Anything you'd be embarrassed to say to a friend's face comes out.

This catches things no checklist does. Do not skip it because the draft looks fine.
