# Style rules

## Punctuation

**No em dashes.** This is the rule the skill exists to enforce. An em dash is almost always one of three things in disguise:

| What it is doing | Replace with |
| --- | --- |
| Joining two complete thoughts | A period. Two sentences. |
| Setting off an aside | Commas, or parentheses if it is genuinely parenthetical. |
| Introducing an explanation or a list | A colon. |

En dashes in number ranges are fine, but a range in a letter usually reads better spelled out: "two to three days" rather than "2 to 3 days".

## Phrases to cut

These mark a letter as generic. None of them survive a draft.

- "I am writing to express my interest in"
- "I believe I would be a great fit"
- "passionate about"
- "proven track record"
- "leverage my skills"
- "team player", "detail-oriented", "results-driven"
- "I would love the opportunity to"
- "Thank you for your time and consideration" as a closing paragraph. "I would be glad to talk" does the same work in a quarter of the space.

## What replaces them

A number, a name, or a thing that happened. "I automated roughly 5,000 rental-scam tickets in two months" carries more than any adjective. If a sentence has no proper noun and no number in it, ask whether it is load-bearing.

## Register

Pick one and hold it for the whole letter. Mixing "I'm" and "I am" across paragraphs is the most common tell that a letter was assembled rather than written. Formal contraction-free reads better in a letter that will be read next to a resume.

## Readable output (Karpathy, Oct 2026)

Write about 80% of the way to Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100). It sets a ceiling on complexity. It is not a template, so keep the register above.

- Keep most sentences to 20 words or fewer, with one idea each.
- Use active voice and simple tenses: "I built", not "I have been building". Use plain words: "use", not "utilize"; "help", not "facilitate".
- Use one name for one thing. Copy the team name and the exact role title from the posting, and repeat them as written. If the opening says "Payments Platform team", do not switch to "the platform group". Numbers follow the same rule (see Numbers).
- The letter stays text. Link a small artifact only if the user asks for one or already has one. Examples: a one-page HTML or a short demo video. Put the link once, next to the claim it proves.

Example. Before: "Over the past few months, I have been leveraging automation to streamline the handling of a large volume of rental-scam tickets." After: "I automated roughly 5,000 rental-scam tickets in two months."

## The paragraph that matters

The second paragraph is the letter. Everything else is scaffolding around it. It should name one thing the user built, attach the real number, and then say what the work taught them, phrased as the exact discipline the job posting asks for. If that paragraph is weak, tightening the other four will not save the letter.

## Honest gaps

When the posting asks for experience the user does not have, say so in one clause and then say what they bring instead. A named gap reads as confidence. An unnamed gap reads as an omission the reader will find anyway.

## Numbers

Use the same phrasing as the resume, character for character. If the resume says "100k worth of paint orders", the letter says "100k worth of paint orders". When a metric is ambiguous, meaning a reader could take a count for a dollar figure, keep the resume's wording in the document and raise the ambiguity with the user in chat rather than silently picking one.
